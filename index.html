BVER: Bidirectional Incremental Voronoi-biased Exploration Curriculum for Reinforcement Learning

A curriculum for sparse-reward, long-horizon tasks that grows start states outward from the goal and goals outward from the start at the same time, and steers the two toward each other.

Anonymous authors · paper under double-blind review

Code GitHub Interactive 2D demo

Give a reinforcement learning agent a long task and a reward only at the very end, and it will almost never see that reward. A quadruped that is rewarded for standing on top of a box, but not for anything on the way there, has no signal to learn from.

In legged locomotion and manipulation this is usually worked around with reference motions, hand-designed curricula, or shaped rewards. Each of these needs a demonstration or engineering that is specific to one task. Automatic curricula avoid that by generating easier intermediate tasks during training, but existing methods grow from one side only: they propose goals for a fixed start, or start states for a fixed goal. The frontier then has to cover the whole distance to the other end by itself.

BVER takes two ideas from sampling-based motion planning. RRT extends the tree node closest to a uniform random sample, which pushes growth into large unexplored regions (the Voronoi bias). RRT-Connect grows one tree from the start and one from the goal until they meet. BVER applies both to curriculum generation: it grows intermediate start states out of the goal and intermediate goals out of the initial state distribution, biases both toward unexplored parts of the task space, pulls them toward each other, and trains a single goal-conditioned policy on both kinds of episode.

We test it with sparse rewards on thirteen simulated tasks: point-mass mazes, quadrupedal locomotion with ANYmal D, and ring-on-peg transfer with a Franka arm. Without a curriculum, PPO rarely or never solves them. BVER converges faster than the other reference-free curricula on every task except the simplest maze. On box climbing it reaches 95% success in about 65% fewer iterations than the best of them, and the resulting policy keeps working when the start, the goal, or the initial heading is changed.

01 Method

Two frontiers, grown by random walks and pulled together

BVER keeps, for each direction, a finite set of states from which the starts or goals of the next training iteration are drawn. The sets grow by random walks, and the policy's own returns decide which states stay.

Backwards expansion
As in reverse curriculum generation (RC), training starts from states found by simulating random actions from a known goal state. After each iteration, every start state is scored by the normalised return R of its rollout. States with R ≤ Rmin are too hard for now and are dropped. States with Rmin < R < Rmax form the frontier, and states with R ≥ Rmax are solved. Frontier and solved states are mixed at a fixed ratio, so the training set holds mostly tasks that are neither too hard nor too easy.
Voronoi-biased seeding
To pick where the next random walks start, BVER samples points uniformly from the task space 𝒯 and takes the nearest neighbour of each sample in the mixed set. A state is then chosen with probability proportional to the volume of its Voronoi cell. That volume is largest at the edge of the explored region, so the expansion moves outward, toward the initial distribution, instead of refining what it already covers. Each walk then takes TB random actions, and the states along the walk become the next candidates.
Forwards expansion
The same mechanism runs with start and goal swapped. The policy starts from s0 ~ ρ0 and is commanded to intermediate goals gi on the forwards frontier. Here the walks are seeded in states the policy can reach, so the forwards sets store the final state of each rollout, which ends within the goal tolerance when the goal was reached and held.
Connecting the two frontiers
Following RRT-Connect, some of the uniform samples are replaced by samples from the other direction's solved set. Intermediate goals then grow toward states from which g★ is already reachable, and intermediate starts grow toward ρ0. The connect ratio pconnect balances this against coverage; we use 0.5 unless stated otherwise.
One policy for both directions
A single goal-conditioned PPO policy πθ(a | s, g) is trained on (s0, gi) and (si, g★) episodes, with the budget split evenly. Part of the parallel environments explore the new random-walk states; the rest keep training on the mixed set, which also replays solved states against forgetting.

  5. Dd ← Rollout(π, Cd) ∪ Rollout(π, ℐd)▹ explore Cd, exploit ℐd

The interactive demo runs this loop on a small 2D grid world. You can draw your own maze, step through one iteration phase by phase, and race forward-only, backward-only and bidirectional expansion against each other.

What BVER assumes. Like RC, it needs a simulator that can be reset to arbitrary states and stepped with random actions, samples from the initial distribution, one known state that achieves the goal, and a bounded task space that contains the target task and can be sampled uniformly.

03 Results

Faster than the other reference-free curricula, and more robust

Sample efficiency

We compare against PPO without a curriculum and a random curriculum that samples starts and goals uniformly from the free task space. The comparison also includes reverse curriculum generation (RC) and two methods that need a demonstration, reference state initialisation (RSI) and Backplay, marked with ★ in the plots. Only the reward band, the random-walk length and the environment split are tuned per domain.

Robustness on box climbing

The box-climbing policies are evaluated under three kinds of variation: perturbed initial yaw, start positions away from the initial distribution (goal fixed at g★), and goals away from the target (start from ρ0). The fourth axis shows sample efficiency as time to 90% success.

BVER's policy solves the task under all three variations. RSI, Backplay and RC train along a single demonstration or a single backwards frontier, so they only cover a narrow band of starts and goals.

Path diversity

This terrain has three different routes to one goal: stairs in the middle, pillars on one side, a ramp on the other. The initial distribution spans the full width, so each route is the closest one for about a third of the starts.

Here we add a potential-based shaping term so that vanilla PPO finds a solution at all; BVER's reward band still uses only the sparse term. Vanilla PPO collapses onto the ramp from every start. BVER's policy uses all three routes: 64% of successful episodes take the stairs, 21% the pillars and 15% the ramp. We attribute this to the coverage-driven expansion, which keeps the curriculum active across the whole task space.

Ablations

Expansion direction. We run forwards and backwards expansion on their own and compare them to the bidirectional version. On box climbing, both directions together are best for both box heights, going up and going down. Of the two single directions, the one that starts on top of the box does better, likely because gravity makes random walks down the edge more likely than walks up it. In the U-maze all variants converge; in the N-maze and large maze the bidirectional version is faster. In ring-on-peg transfer, each single direction spreads from its own peg into the space between but does not reliably reach the other peg; only the combination gets to 100%.

Connect ratio. On the three mazes, drawing part of the seeding samples from the other direction's solved set (pconnect = 0.5 or 1) improves sample efficiency over uniform samples only (pconnect = 0). The two nonzero settings perform about the same.

04 Limitations

What BVER needs, and what we have not tested

BVER needs a simulator that can be reset to arbitrary states, random-action rollouts, and a bounded task space with a meaningful distance. All task spaces here are two- or three-dimensional position boxes with a Euclidean metric, the regime where RRT-style expansion is known to work well. Higher-dimensional or non-Euclidean task spaces are untested; learned distances could help there.

The backwards expansion also assumes roughly reversible dynamics. A random walk from the goal reaching a state does not mean the goal can be reached from that state, as gravity on the box edge shows. Such states are only dropped once a rollout has shown them to be unsolvable. Learned dynamics models or rollouts of the current policy could replace the random walks. Finally, all results are in simulation; running the policies on hardware is future work.
